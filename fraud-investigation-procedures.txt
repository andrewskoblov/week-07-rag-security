FRAUD INVESTIGATION PROCEDURES AND CASE MANAGEMENT GUIDE
Financial Institution Internal Compliance Operations
Fraud Investigation Unit — Standard Operating Procedures

=============================================================
SECTION 1: PURPOSE AND SCOPE
=============================================================

This document establishes the standard operating procedures for conducting fraud investigations within a financial institution's Fraud Investigation Unit (FIU). These procedures apply to all cases of suspected internal fraud, external fraud, identity theft, account takeover, check fraud, wire fraud, elder financial exploitation, and related financial crimes affecting the institution or its customers.

The goals of a fraud investigation are to: (1) determine whether fraud occurred; (2) identify the perpetrators and their methods; (3) quantify the financial loss; (4) recover funds where possible; (5) preserve evidence for potential criminal prosecution or civil litigation; (6) comply with regulatory reporting obligations including SAR filing; and (7) identify control failures and recommend remediation to prevent recurrence.

All investigations must be conducted in a manner that preserves the integrity of the evidence, protects the rights of all parties, maintains confidentiality, and complies with applicable laws and regulations.

=============================================================
SECTION 2: CASE INITIATION
=============================================================

2.1 Sources of Referral

Fraud investigations may be initiated based on referrals from any of the following sources:

- Transaction monitoring system alerts generated by the institution's AML/fraud detection software
- Branch staff or relationship managers reporting suspicious customer behavior
- Customer complaints alleging unauthorized transactions, account takeover, or identity theft
- Internal audit findings indicating potential irregularities
- Law enforcement requests or grand jury subpoenas
- Regulatory examiner referrals during BSA/AML examinations
- Reports from other financial institutions via information sharing under Section 314(b) of the USA PATRIOT Act
- Anonymous tips submitted through the institution's internal ethics hotline

2.2 Initial Assessment

Upon receiving a referral, the assigned investigator must conduct an initial assessment within 24–48 hours to determine the scope and priority of the case. The initial assessment includes:

- Reviewing the referral details and the nature of the alleged fraud
- Pulling account transaction history and identifying the timeframe of suspicious activity
- Running the subject's name and account information against internal watchlists and prior SAR filings
- Checking the subject against OFAC sanctions lists and adverse media databases
- Determining the estimated financial exposure to the institution and its customers
- Assigning a case priority level (Critical, High, Medium, Low) based on the dollar amount, ongoing nature of the activity, and regulatory implications

2.3 Case File Creation

Every investigation must have a case file created in the institution's case management system. The case file must include: a unique case number; the date the investigation was opened; the referral source; the names, account numbers, and contact information of all subjects; a summary of the alleged activity; and the assigned investigator's name. All subsequent investigative steps, findings, and decisions must be documented in the case file in real time.

=============================================================
SECTION 3: EVIDENCE COLLECTION AND PRESERVATION
=============================================================

3.1 Types of Evidence

Fraud investigations rely on multiple categories of evidence:

Financial Records: Account statements, transaction records, wire transfer confirmations, check images, loan documents, wire transfer logs, currency transaction reports, and ACH records. These should be pulled directly from the institution's core banking system and preserved as certified copies.

Electronic Evidence: Email correspondence, online banking logs, IP addresses, device fingerprints, login timestamps, and any electronic communications related to the fraudulent activity. Electronic evidence must be collected using forensically sound methods that preserve metadata and maintain chain of custody.

Documentary Evidence: Identification documents, account opening forms, signature cards, loan applications, and any other physical or electronic documents used in the commission of the fraud or relevant to identifying the perpetrator.

Witness Statements: Statements from branch employees, relationship managers, customers, or other witnesses who observed or have knowledge of the suspicious activity. Statements should be documented in writing and signed by the witness.

3.2 Chain of Custody

Maintaining a proper chain of custody is essential, particularly in cases that may result in criminal prosecution. Every piece of evidence must be logged with: a description of the item; the date, time, and location it was collected; the name of the person who collected it; and a record of every person who subsequently handled or reviewed it.

Evidence must be stored securely in a dedicated evidence repository with access restricted to authorized personnel only. Physical documents should be stored in sealed, labeled evidence envelopes. Electronic evidence should be stored on write-protected media or in a secure, access-controlled digital repository.

Do not alter, annotate, or modify any evidence. Do not print emails and discard originals. Do not conduct investigation activities in a way that tips off the subject, alerts them to the investigation, or allows them to destroy evidence.

3.3 Legal Holds

When a fraud investigation has the potential to result in litigation or criminal prosecution, a legal hold must be issued through the institution's legal department. A legal hold suspends the routine destruction of documents, emails, and records related to the case and requires all personnel who may have relevant materials to preserve them until the hold is lifted. Failure to comply with a legal hold can result in spoliation sanctions in court proceedings.

=============================================================
SECTION 4: INVESTIGATIVE TECHNIQUES
=============================================================

4.1 Transaction Analysis

The foundation of most financial fraud investigations is a detailed analysis of account transactions. Investigators should:

- Map all transactions within the relevant timeframe, including deposits, withdrawals, wires, ACH entries, and check activity
- Identify the source of all incoming funds and the destination of all outgoing funds
- Look for patterns consistent with red flag indicators (structuring, layering, round amounts, threshold avoidance)
- Trace fund flows through related accounts and third-party accounts where possible
- Calculate total losses and identify which transactions are confirmed fraudulent versus suspicious

4.2 Link Analysis

Link analysis involves mapping relationships between subjects, accounts, addresses, phone numbers, and other identifiers to identify networks of related individuals or entities. Investigators should check whether:

- Multiple accounts share the same address, phone number, email, or device
- The subject has relationships with known fraud perpetrators or individuals with prior SARs
- Business entities associated with the subject are shell companies, dissolved entities, or companies with no verifiable operations
- IP addresses or devices associated with suspicious logins are linked to other accounts at the institution

4.3 Open Source Intelligence (OSINT)

Investigators may use publicly available information to supplement their investigation, including:

- Court records and criminal history databases
- Corporate filings and Secretary of State records to verify business legitimacy
- Property records to verify real estate ownership claims
- Social media profiles to identify lifestyle inconsistencies or connections to other subjects
- News and media searches for adverse information about the subject
- PACER (Public Access to Court Electronic Records) for federal civil and criminal court filings

4.4 Interviews

Investigator interviews of witnesses and, in appropriate circumstances, subjects should be conducted in accordance with the institution's legal counsel guidelines. Key points:

- Witness interviews should be conducted before the subject is aware of the investigation
- Prepare a list of specific questions based on transaction analysis findings before the interview
- Document all interviews in writing; obtain a written statement from the witness if possible
- Do not interview the subject of the investigation without first consulting legal counsel
- If the subject is an employee, coordinate with Human Resources before any confrontational interview

=============================================================
SECTION 5: SAR FILING DETERMINATION
=============================================================

5.1 Referral to SAR Review Committee

Once the investigation has gathered sufficient evidence, the investigator must prepare a SAR referral memo summarizing the findings and recommending whether to file. The memo should include: a summary of the suspicious activity; the evidence supporting the finding; the identity of all subjects; the dollar amount involved; and the investigator's recommendation on filing.

The SAR Review Committee evaluates the referral and makes the final filing decision. If the committee determines that a SAR should be filed, the investigator must complete the FinCEN SAR form and submit it within the applicable deadline (30 days from initial detection, or 60 days if no suspect was identified at time of detection).

5.2 Parallel Law Enforcement Referral

In cases involving significant financial losses, ongoing criminal activity, or identified suspects, the institution should consider making a parallel referral to law enforcement in addition to filing the SAR. Law enforcement referrals can be made to the FBI, Secret Service, Postal Inspection Service, IRS Criminal Investigation, or local law enforcement depending on the nature of the fraud. Coordination with legal counsel is required before making a direct law enforcement referral.

=============================================================
SECTION 6: CASE CLOSURE AND REPORTING
=============================================================

6.1 Case Closure Criteria

A case may be closed when: the investigation is complete and a SAR has been filed (or a documented decision not to file has been made); all evidence has been secured; affected accounts have been remediated or closed; losses have been quantified and reported; and any applicable law enforcement referrals have been made.

6.2 Post-Investigation Reporting

Upon closure, the investigator must prepare a final case report documenting: the nature of the fraud; the investigation methodology; the evidence collected; the financial losses; regulatory actions taken (SAR filings); law enforcement referrals; account actions taken; and recommendations for control improvements.

The final report is reviewed by the Fraud Investigation Unit manager and the Chief Compliance Officer. Significant findings are escalated to the Board of Directors or its Risk Committee as required by the institution's governance framework.

6.3 Control Remediation

Every closed fraud case should result in a lessons-learned review to identify whether the fraud exploited a gap in the institution's controls. Identified control failures must be documented and referred to the appropriate business line or operations team for remediation. The Fraud Investigation Unit should track remediation progress and report status to compliance management quarterly.
